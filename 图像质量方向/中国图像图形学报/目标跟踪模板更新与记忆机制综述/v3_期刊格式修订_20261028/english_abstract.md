**Objective** Visual single-object tracking estimates the location of an initially specified target throughout a video while preserving its identity. The initial observation provides a reliable identity anchor, but its appearance can become outdated under deformation, viewpoint changes, occlusion, illumination variation, and interference from similar objects. Updating the target representation may improve adaptation, yet an update based on an incorrect prediction can introduce background information and cause persistent drift. Template updating and temporal memory therefore address a common problem: how to retain useful history without allowing unreliable observations to dominate future decisions. This review examines these mechanisms through the relationships among appearance adaptation, identity preservation, and computational cost. Its primary scope is RGB single-object tracking initialized by a bounding box in the first frame. Related research on video object segmentation, language-conditioned tracking, and multimodal tracking is considered when it clarifies memory organization or evaluation boundaries, while differences in task definitions and supervision are retained.

**Method** The review uses a problem-oriented narrative synthesis rather than claiming exhaustive coverage of every database or publication venue. Bibliographic records are assembled from official conference proceedings, journal websites, publisher metadata, and corresponding author releases. A formal publication and its preprint are treated as one research contribution. Templates, historical samples, explicit memory, recurrent states, and model parameters are first distinguished according to what information they retain and how they are modified. A shared analytical framework then describes the causal loop from historical retrieval and current localization to candidate construction, reliability assessment, memory writing, and capacity management. Within this framework, template updating is compared along three dimensions: when an update is triggered, how new and existing representations are combined, and how potentially contaminated observations are rejected or corrected. Memory mechanisms are compared according to stored content, temporal organization, selection and replacement rules, and the operation used to retrieve history. The analysis also separates online parameter optimization from updates to cached features or recurrent states. This distinction prevents different forms of adaptation from being conflated solely because they all change during tracking. Representative benchmarks and evaluation protocols are reviewed alongside the mechanisms. Quantitative discussion uses traceable results reported in published papers and within-paper ablation studies. Differences in training data, backbone capacity, search regions, input resolution, and runtime measurement are treated as potential confounding factors. Values reproduced in tables and plots are literature results; they are not measurements from a newly implemented, unified benchmark experiment.

**Result** The comparison identifies several recurring design relationships. First, update timing and update content are inseparable. A confidence threshold can reduce the number of unreliable writes, but its usefulness depends on whether the confidence signal reflects instance identity, localization quality, or only similarity to the current representation. A confident match to a distractor may still be an unsuitable update. Second, retaining an initial template can stabilize identity, whereas dynamic templates provide coverage of appearances that were absent from the first frame. Their interaction requires an explicit balance between anchoring and adaptation. Third, larger memories provide additional observations but can also accumulate redundant or misleading information. The usefulness of stored history depends on selection and retrieval, not only on the number of entries. Published ablations of reference-frame counts and update intervals illustrate that increasing historical coverage or update frequency does not guarantee higher accuracy. These observations should remain bounded by the particular models and datasets in which they were obtained. Fourth, explicit memories and compressed recurrent states offer different forms of control. Explicit entries can support selection, weighting, replacement, and inspection, while recurrent states aggregate history through repeated transformations. Compression may reduce storage, but it can make the contribution of an individual observation and the recovery from a harmful update less transparent. Neither representation is universally preferable without specifying the computational budget and target scenario. Fifth, historical information cannot compensate for every failure in candidate generation. If the target falls outside a restricted search region, even an accurate appearance memory may fail to restore localization. Long-term recovery consequently requires attention to search expansion, re-detection, and identity verification as well as memory quality. Finally, evaluation protocols constrain the meaning of reported improvements. Dataset-level overlap scores summarize localization performance but do not directly identify whether gains arise from more reliable writes, more diverse memories, stronger retrieval, or a more capable backbone. Comparisons that mix task protocols, initialization information, or computational settings can therefore obscure the contribution of the memory mechanism itself. Mechanism-specific evidence is most informative when supported by controlled ablations, consistent evaluation settings, and clearly stated failure conditions. The review also distinguishes historical appearance from motion and semantic context. Appearance entries describe how a target has looked, motion states constrain where it may be found, and semantic information can support recognition without uniquely identifying an instance. Treating these signals as interchangeable can obscure the source of robustness. Related segmentation and multimodal methods offer useful ideas for memory selection and readout, but transferring them to bounding-box tracking requires accounting for different outputs, prompts, annotation costs, and sensor assumptions. This separation helps organize related work without merging incomparable performance claims.

**Conclusion** Template updating and temporal memory are best analyzed as interacting stages of an online information-processing loop. Reliable tracking requires more than frequent updates or large historical buffers: it requires observations that preserve target identity, representations that capture useful variation, and retrieval operations that exploit the retained information under realistic computational constraints. Several research directions follow from this synthesis. Update decisions should consider the expected future value of an observation rather than relying exclusively on current localization confidence. Memory systems should expose ways to detect contamination, reduce the influence of harmful entries, and recover a trusted state. Resource-aware designs should jointly evaluate accuracy, latency, storage, and the growth of retrieval cost with history length. Evaluation should distinguish failures caused by localization, search coverage, writing, retention, and reading, and should report whether corrupted history continues to affect later frames. Controlled experiments that vary one mechanism while holding other components fixed would provide stronger evidence than aggregate rankings alone. These directions provide a basis for subsequent implementation and empirical testing. The present review supplies an organizational framework and a critical reading of published evidence, while claims about causal superiority or statistical significance remain limited by the experimental controls available in the original studies.
